We put a lot of effort into making healthcare websites and applications conform to WCAG. We audit them, document defects, fix what we find and test again. All of that work matters, and I wouldn't suggest for a moment that we should stop doing it.

But I worry about what happens when an organization starts treating WCAG conformance as proof that its healthcare services are accessible.

A hospital has a patient portal that meets WCAG 2.2 AA. A blind patient uses it with their screen reader to book an appointment and complete the necessary forms. At the clinic, they're told to complete another form on a touchscreen they can't use. The patient has to ask someone else to enter their private medical information.

The portal's accessibility assessment didn't account for what would happen at the clinic. Who takes responsibility for accessibility when the barrier is outside the system that was assessed?

## What WCAG establishes and where it stops

WCAG provides requirements for assessing web content and applications. It's an essential part of digital accessibility, including in healthcare.

The scope of that assessment matters. The touchscreen is another digital interface, even though the patient encounters it during an in-person visit. Assessing the portal tells us nothing about whether that interface works for disabled patients.

There are also accessibility problems that WCAG cannot adequately address.

A Deaf patient requests an interpreter when booking an appointment. The scheduling application records the request, but the information never reaches the clinic. Nobody has arranged an interpreter when the patient arrives.

Another patient requests written communication, but appointment changes arrive through automated telephone calls.

The applications could pass WCAG assessments while the organization continues to create barriers through the way it handles patient information.

## Following the patient through healthcare

### Where systems meet

Healthcare organizations use separate systems for scheduling, registration, identity verification, telehealth and patient records.

For a virtual consultation, the video platform might be accessible. But the patient has to complete identity verification through another application before joining. If the identity verification application isn't accessible, they never reach the consultation.

The identity verification application has its own WCAG problem. What concerns me is how easily that problem can be missed when an organization assesses each product separately.

A vendor's Accessibility Conformance Report describes one product, not whether the entire process works.

Each team can believe it has fulfilled its responsibilities while the patient is unable to get care.

### Healthcare isn't like buying a toaster

If an online store is inaccessible, I can abandon my purchase and buy a toaster somewhere else.

Healthcare is different. Someone who needs an appointment or diagnostic test needs care now. They can't simply choose another hospital or specialist because registration is inaccessible. Pain, fatigue and illness can make the process harder.

Time limits are one example. WCAG 2.2 Success Criterion 2.2.1 addresses timing, including ways to extend time limits and certain exceptions. A medical questionnaire could satisfy that criterion by warning users and allowing them to extend their session, yet still be difficult for someone who needs frequent breaks because of their symptoms.

The timing of communication matters too. A hospital sends accessible instructions for a procedure, but they arrive too late for someone to arrange accessible transportation or personal support. The patient misses their appointment.

### When the alternative creates another barrier

"Call us if you can't use the form."

I've seen this offered as an accessibility solution far too often.

For someone with a speech disability who cannot use the telephone, it's no solution at all. Other patients may have to ask family members to help with private medical information, or wait until office hours to do something everyone else can do online.

An alternative should offer comparable independence and privacy without unnecessary delay.

Otherwise, we're asking disabled patients to accept a poorer service.

## People, communication and information

### What staff can actually do

Having several communication channels doesn't mean every patient can use them. A Deaf patient who needs an interpreter may not communicate effectively through written notes, and someone who relies on email may be unable to use the telephone.

Recording a communication preference is only useful if the clinic acts on it.

I don't think every failure should be blamed on the employee. The receptionist insisting on the touchscreen may simply be following instructions. Without a procedure for someone who cannot use it, the organization has failed both the employee and the patient.

Staff need procedures and authority to arrange accommodations.

### Stop making patients explain everything again

Disabled patients often explain their accessibility requirements when booking, repeat them during registration and explain them again to the clinician. At the next appointment, they start over.

We already record a great deal about patients. Their accessibility requirements should be included in a form staff can use. Record what someone needs rather than relying on a diagnosis. Knowing that a patient is Deaf says little about how they communicate.

Patients should be able to review and update this information. An interpreter request must reach the clinic arranging the appointment.

Sharing information between healthcare organizations raises consent and privacy questions. Those questions have to be addressed so patients aren't repeatedly forced to negotiate access to care.

### Disabled healthcare professionals matter too

Accessibility assessments tend to concentrate on patient-facing technology. We also have to examine what disabled healthcare professionals use.

Electronic health records and internal tools can create barriers for disabled staff.

A disabled clinician might use the patient portal but have difficulty accessing the electronic health record needed to treat the patient. The systems may belong to different teams.

I don't see how an organization can claim to have addressed accessibility across its healthcare services while overlooking the technology its own disabled staff depend on.

## Assessing the healthcare service

Looking beyond individual systems changes what healthcare organizations have to assess.

Assess complete patient journeys with disabled people, from booking through follow-up.

Measure the additional work disabled patients do to receive care. An appointment might be completed only after the patient spent hours making telephone calls, asked someone else to complete a form or repeatedly explained their requirements. If we measure only whether the appointment happened, we miss much of their experience.

Assign responsibility for accessibility across the service, including handoffs between departments and the way applications work together.

When purchasing technology, assess how it works with existing systems. Vendor conformance reports are useful evidence, but testing must also cover the service those products create together.

These are organizational responsibilities. They cannot all be delegated to a vendor or an accessibility testing team.

## Who takes responsibility?

The blind patient who booked an appointment through an accessible portal still had to ask someone else to complete a form at the clinic.

The portal team can say its application passed WCAG testing. The clinic can say staff followed the registration procedure. Both statements might be true.

Healthcare organizations have to take responsibility for accessibility throughout the services they provide, including the technology their disabled staff use to deliver care.

That patient should never have had to surrender their independence and privacy to receive care.
